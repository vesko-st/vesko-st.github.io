For most of my career, the workhorse of applied NLP was not a chatbot but a classifier — a model trained to answer one narrow question and return a label. Frontier chat models have become so capable, and so convenient, that it is now tempting to route every task through them, including tasks that are, at their core, a choice among a handful of options. I wanted to understand what that convenience really costs, and whether a small, purpose-built classifier can still hold its own on accuracy.

Lately everyone has been talking about Jev, and I wanted to put it through its paces. Jev is TypeSafe's System One _Choice_ primitive — a compact model that takes a passage and a question, is given a set of options, and returns exactly one of them. That is precisely the shape of a multiple-choice benchmark, so I set up a head-to-head against the current frontier Claude models: Haiku 4.5, Sonnet 5, and Opus 5.

There is a wrinkle in any comparison like this: the public benchmarks we reach for by default may already sit in the models' pretraining data, and the headline numbers we remember are often a generation out of date. So I did two things. First, I re-ran every model myself under a single fixed protocol rather than trusting stale leaderboards. Second, I added two hedges against contamination — MMLU-CF, an explicitly contamination-free variant of MMLU, and a small corpus I wrote by hand over a single long article, which I describe later.
